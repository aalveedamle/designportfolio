Case Study 06 · AI-Native

The best AI in this product is the AI you never see.

Yulu — an AI-native micro-mobility product designed from a real 2020 research base. Four narrow agents that prevent failures instead of apologising for them.

ContextDelhi Micro-Mobility
RoleAI Design Engineer
ScopeEvidence → Prompts → Prototype
Year2026 · from 2020 research
23 rider interviews in the evidence base this was designed from — real research, conducted in Delhi in 2020.
4 narrow agents in the architecture. Only one of them is a language model — and that restraint is the design decision.
3 system-prompt versions before the billing agent behaved. V1 invented refunds that didn't exist.
01 — The framing

Most products add AI at the end. This one couldn't have been.

There is a version of this project that takes the Yulu app as it exists, bolts a chat assistant into the corner, and calls it AI-native. It would have been faster to design, easier to demo, and completely beside the point.

The research said riders were losing trust because the system surprised them — a bike that looked available but wouldn't unlock, a lock that read as closed but wasn't, a battery that died two kilometres from the office, a billing dispute with no evidence trail. None of those are conversation problems. A chatbot is a well-designed apology for a failure that should have been prevented.

So the question I actually designed against was narrower and harder: what can a system predict, decide and resolve on the rider's behalf, before she ever has to notice it went wrong? That question has an answer only if AI sits underneath the product rather than on top of it — which means the interaction model, the failure states, the billing logic and the interface all have to be designed around it from the first screen.

This case study is the working. It includes the parts that usually get edited out: the direction I rejected, the system prompt that produced garbage, the code the model got wrong, and the evaluation that caught it.

What is real here, and what is a design exploration

Real: the 2020 research base — a system study, an observational field study at two Delhi metro stations, 23 rider interviews, four personas, two customer journey maps, a SWOT and a gap analysis. That work was done as a team of three (Aalvee Damle, Bhanuja Dass, Vrinda Singh) for a service design course, and its findings are quoted here unchanged.

A design exploration: everything after it. The product concept, the agent architecture, the system prompts, the interface, the prototype and the evaluation are mine, built in 2026, and were never shipped to a live fleet. Where I report evaluation results, they come from scripted scenarios run against my own prototype — directional, not empirical. I have labelled them that way throughout rather than dressing them up as product metrics.

02 — The evidence

I started with evidence, not with a model.

Six months of shared micro-mobility in Delhi, studied from the inside: the system, the street, and 23 people who had just tried it for the first time.

23personal interviews, mostly first-time riders — the brief was deliberately "staying a beginner".
2Yulu zones observed in the field: INA and Mandi House metro stations, including conversations with ground staff.
4personas and 2 full customer journey maps, spanning past experience through post-ride.
26SWOT entries and 6 named gaps — the raw material the synthesis had to work across.

Two things in this evidence base mattered more than the rest, and both are easy to miss on a first read.

The first is where dissatisfaction actually clustered. The journey map's "after usage" stage lists the complaints riders gave unprompted: storage space, roads, poor infrastructure, "app could be smooth", battery life, cost. Read as a list, it looks like six equal problems. Read against the interviews and the system study's feedback loop — less battery, broken cycle parts, abandoned cycles, poor locking system — three of those six are things the product cannot control, and three are reliability failures the product causes.

The second is that price had already been accepted. The persona work describes riders as "price-conscious but not a constraint". Delhi's roads and infrastructure showed up as background conditions people had made peace with. What they hadn't made peace with was a system that behaved unpredictably.

03 — Interrogating the evidence

I used AI to read the research against me, not for me.

The risk with a corpus you already know is that you find what you expect. So I gave the model the whole thing at once, and wrote the prompt to make agreement expensive.

# ROLE
You are a research analyst. You are not a designer and you are not
proposing solutions. If you propose a solution I will discard the
whole response.

# INPUT
Below is the complete research corpus for a 2020 study of Yulu
shared e-bikes in Delhi: a system study, field observation notes,
23 interview summaries, 4 personas, 2 customer journey maps, a
SWOT, and a gap list. <corpus>...</corpus>

# TASK
1. Separate SAID from INFERRED. For every finding, mark whether a
   participant stated it or the research team concluded it. I need
   to know which of these has a person behind it.
2. Cluster the complaints by underlying cause, not by topic. Two
   complaints belong together if fixing one would affect the other.
3. For each cluster give me: frequency of mention across the corpus,
   and whether it damages trust in the system or merely
   satisfaction with the experience. Those are different failures.
4. Name every cluster that is outside the product's control
   (infrastructure, city policy, hardware economics) and set it aside.
5. Tell me what the corpus is thin on. Where would you refuse to
   draw a conclusion, and what would you have asked instead?

# CONSTRAINTS
- Quote the source artefact for every claim. No claim without a source.
- Do not soften findings that contradict the 2020 team's own
  recommendation. Argue against it if the evidence does.
- If two artefacts disagree, say so rather than averaging them.
Why the prompt looks like this

Every clause is doing a job. "You are not proposing solutions" exists because the first run without it produced a feature list within two paragraphs — and once a model has proposed a solution, everything after it is written to justify that solution. "Separate said from inferred" exists because the 2020 deck mixes the two, and a synthesis that inherits that blur will confidently report a design team's opinion as a user need. "Cluster by underlying cause" is the instruction that produced the actual reframe. "Set aside what's outside the product's control" is scope discipline the model won't apply on its own — left alone it will happily recommend improving Delhi's roads.

The last clause is the one I'd defend hardest in a critique. Asking the model where the evidence is thin is the only part of the prompt that produces something I can't get from re-reading the deck myself.

Kept — C1 and C2, and this is the reframe

Clustering by cause rather than topic is what turned six equal-looking complaints into one structural problem. "App could be smooth", "weak tracking system", "poor locking system" and "payment doesn't go through" are the same failure wearing four costumes: the rider cannot tell what state the system is in. That is the sentence the rest of the project is built on, and I did not have it before this pass.

Rewritten — C3 was right about the contradiction, wrong about the conclusion

The model spotted something I'd read past for weeks: the 2020 team's own fix introduces a cash handover at the bike, and the same corpus records cash handling as friction. That catch is real and I kept it. But the model framed the cluster as "money handled by strangers," which points at removing the human. The interviews don't support that — ground staff show up in the field notes as the thing that rescues a failed ride. I rewrote the cluster as "charges the rider can't evidence," which points at the dispute, not the person.

Rejected — the second synthesis run

Run two of the same prompt produced a fifth cluster: "safety and confidence on the road," built on the journey map's "am I safe on it", "don't want to fall", and "how should I sit on it". It reads plausibly and it is the kind of finding that sounds humane in a portfolio.

It comes from a single journey-map cell. There are no interview quotes behind it, no field observations, and no SWOT entry. The model had synthesised a theme out of one artefact's speculative language — precisely the "said vs inferred" failure the prompt was written to prevent, appearing anyway. I dropped it, and I take the fact that it appeared as the argument for reading model output as a proposal rather than a finding.

Kept, and it constrained the whole project — the thinness note

"You would not draw retention conclusions from this data" is correct, and it is inconvenient, because retention is exactly the problem I wanted to design against. It did not change my direction; it changed what I am allowed to claim. Everywhere below that this case study talks about retention, it is stated as a hypothesis with a defined test rather than as a finding — and Section 12 defines the measurement that would settle it.

The problem was never supply or price. It was invisible failures eroding trust — and the rider carrying the cost of the system's uncertainty.
04 — Choosing a direction

The obvious AI product here was a chatbot. I killed it.

Four directions came out of the opportunity map — one of them mine, one of them the model's, and the one everybody expects. They were scored against criteria I wrote before I looked at the options.

Chosen — designed and prototyped below
Why H3 died — and it is the most important decision in this project

A visible chatbot competes with what the research says the rider actually wants. Ananya does not want a better way to report that her bike is broken. She wants to not get a broken bike. Every conversational turn in that concept is a turn that exists because the system already failed.

It also fails a test I hold AI features to: does the interaction cost less than the problem it solves? A rider standing at a dead bike in Delhi traffic, typing to an assistant, is paying attention she doesn't have to a system that should have spent that attention for her. The chat surface is the demo-friendly option and the wrong one.

Where the model earned its place — and where I overruled it

I asked for adversarial alternatives after I'd written my three. It produced a fifth direction I hadn't considered: crowd-sourced bike health — riders rate the bike after each ride, and those ratings drive what gets shown. It's a genuinely good idea and cheap to build.

I rejected it, for a reason worth stating: it moves the labour of maintaining trust back onto the rider, and it fails exactly when it matters most. The rider whose lock jammed is the least likely to stop and file a rating; the rider who had a clean ride has no reason to. You get a survivor-biased signal precisely on the bikes you need truth about. The idea did leave a mark though — it's why "Report an issue" during a ride attaches telemetry automatically instead of asking the rider to describe anything.

05 — The hypothesis

Design for the rider who is two bad rides from leaving.

The product hypothesis

If bike readiness, trip feasibility and billing fairness are resolved before the rider encounters them, trust becomes a property of the system rather than something the rider has to actively maintain — and daily-rider retention improves without any change to price or fleet size.

That is a claim, not a finding, and the synthesis already told me the 2020 corpus can't settle it: every participant was a beginner. So the hypothesis carries a defined test with it, and Section 12 specifies the instrumentation that would run it.

The design target, and what it costs

The research contains two usable archetypes — the Office Goer (urban commuter, under 5 km, hassling with auto drivers) and the Daily Life Traveller (travelling to the CBD with multiple stops). I designed for a composite of those two, and deliberately not for the price-sensitive occasional rider who dominates the persona set.

The cost of that choice is real and I'd rather name it than hide it. Three of the four 2020 personas are students; two had used Yulu once or never. Optimising for the daily rider means the onboarding is thin, there's no value-proposition carousel, and nothing in the product works to convince a sceptic. That is a deliberate trade, made because retention — keeping a daily user from quietly churning after her second bad ride — is the harder problem and the one that rewards systems thinking over onboarding polish. It is also the assumption most worth testing against a second persona later.

06 — Division of labour

AI generated. I decided.

This is the map I worked to. It isn't a disclaimer — it's the operating model, and every section below can be checked against it.

Me · the designer

Every judgement that determines what the product is and who absorbs the cost of it being wrong.

  • Defined the problem — reframed six complaints into one trust failure, and rejected the model's fifth cluster when the evidence didn't hold it up.
  • Set the constraints — no visible AI surface, no confidence score shown to the rider, no failure that resolves in the company's favour.
  • Chose the product direction — killed the concierge, folded two directions into one, wrote the scoring criteria before seeing the options.
  • Designed the interaction — the absence-based map, the destination-first flow, the placement of payment at the bike rather than at signup.
  • Specified AI behaviour — the asymmetry rule, the escalation contract, the plain-language message style, and what each agent is deliberately not told.
  • Judged quality — wrote the evaluation rubric, scored the outputs, and accepted an over-cautious failure mode as the correct one.
  • Reviewed and modified the code — including the render bug that violated the core product promise and the dead threshold that made a designed state unreachable.

Volume, pattern-finding and first drafts. Nothing on this side ships without passing through the left.

07 — The architecture

Four narrow agents. Only one of them is a language model.

The restraint is the design decision. Most of this system is thresholds and a decay curve — because most of it should be. The model is used where judgement over messy evidence is genuinely required, and nowhere else.

ProductDesignAIEngineering

Bike Readiness

Deterministic · rules
Job
Decide whether a bike appears as available at all — for this trip.
Inputs
Lock-state ping, GPS drift, last successful unlock, days since service, predicted range vs. required range.
Output
{ ok, reasons[] } — never a bare boolean. The reasons array exists because the ops dashboard and the failure states both need to explain themselves.
Why not a model
These are hard signals with clear thresholds. A model here would add latency, cost and non-determinism to a decision that has a correct answer.

Battery Decay

Predictive · regression
Job
Predict whether a bike dies mid-route for this specific trip, not in the abstract.
Inputs
Charge state, the decay curve for that battery's age and cycle count, trip distance, terrain and load assumptions.
Output
Predicted range in km, plus a marginal flag when it clears the requirement by a thin margin.
Why not a model
Battery decay is a physical process with a fitted curve. Asking a language model to do arithmetic over a decay function is the wrong tool wearing a fashionable hat.

Billing Resolution

LLM · reasons over evidence
Job
Resolve lock- and battery-failure disputes without a human, and explain the resolution to the rider in one sentence she can act on.
Inputs
The ride's telemetry: GPS trace, lock events, range curve, fare ledger, and any flags the other two agents raised during that ride.
Output
A structured decision — adjust, no_action or escalate — with an evidence array and a rider-facing message.
Why a model
This is the one job in the system that is genuinely a judgement: incomplete, sometimes contradictory evidence, an open-ended set of ways a ride can go wrong, and an output that has to be written in human language. It is exactly the shape of problem conventional software handles badly and a rules engine handles by escalating everything to a support queue.

Rebalancing / Dispatch

Ranking + LLM rationale
Job
Predict zone under- and oversupply 1–3 hours out, and hand ground staff a ranked task list.
Inputs
Historical demand by zone and time, live distribution, and Readiness flags — a low-confidence bike gets scheduled for pickup, not just a move.
Output
Ranked tasks, each carrying a plain-language reason. The ranking is a model in the statistical sense; the sentence explaining it is the LLM.
Human in the loop
Staff can override — "this bike wasn't actually broken" — and that override feeds back into Readiness confidence for that bike and zone. Visible correction, not a black box.
The other half of the architecture

PricingFixed rate table₹2.00/min riding, ₹0.50/min paused. A rider must be able to predict her fare before she commits. Dynamic pricing would be the single fastest way to undo everything else this product is doing.

The rule I applied

A capability earns a model when its input is incomplete, its output space is open-ended, and a wrong answer is recoverable. Billing adjudication meets all three. Pricing, geofencing and payment state meet none of them. Being able to say why a thing isn't AI is most of what separates an AI-native product from an AI-decorated one.

08 — The interaction

The central question wasn't what the AI knows. It was when the AI should speak.

Three treatments for the same information — how ready a bike is — mocked up side by side and argued out. The one I chose shows the rider nothing.

Chosen
The governing principle this produced

The AI surfaces only when its confidence is low enough that a human needs to make the call. Silence is the default output. A confident bike doesn't justify itself with a number — it should just be there.

The badge from Treatment B isn't discarded, it's relocated. It reappears inside the marginal-trip notice, at the one moment where a genuine judgement call exists and the rider has already committed her attention to a single bike. Three of seventeen screens ever show an agent speaking. The other fourteen are agents working.

Where absence gets dangerous, and what pays for it

Hiding things is a strong move and it has an obvious failure mode: an empty map reads as a broken app, not as a system exercising care. So absence is paid for in exactly two places. The empty state names the check out loud — "There are bikes here, but none have the range for 6.2 km. We'd rather tell you than send you to one that runs out." And the sheet narrates removals when the destination changes — "2 bikes dropped off — they can't make the longer trip" — so a pin disappearing reads as a decision rather than a glitch.

The cost is measured, not assumed: hide rate and empty-map frequency are two of the metrics in Section 12, and they are the numbers that would tune the thresholds if this ran on a real fleet.

The rule change that made the whole flow move

Designing the interaction surfaced a problem in my own architecture: a bike is not "ready" in the abstract. It is ready for a specific trip. A bike with 4 km of range is perfectly ready for a 1 km hop and a liability for a 9 km commute. Any readiness score computed without a destination is either a lie or a lowest-common-denominator guess.

readiness.rule · the number the whole product hangs on
required_range = max(trip_distance × 1.5, 2 km)

bike is READY  ⟺  predicted_range ≥ required_range
                  AND lock state confirmed
                  AND GPS drift within tolerance
                  AND last successful unlock within threshold
                  AND days-since-service within threshold

The 1.5× multiplier covers detours, wrong turns and error in the decay curve itself. The 2 km floor stops a 400 m hop from clearing a nearly-dead bike that would then strand the next rider. Both are tunable parameters, and I've treated them as the two numbers most worth iterating on — they are the dial that trades availability against reliability.

The consequence reshaped the flow: readiness has to be recomputed whenever the destination changes, which means the destination has to come first. The search field that would normally be an optional convenience on the map became the entry point of the entire ride flow — and the Battery Decay agent went from a dormant background service to the thing that decides what you see.

09 — Defining AI behaviour

A behaviour spec first. Then the prompt that enforces it.

Writing the prompt before writing the spec is how you end up with a well-phrased assistant that behaves unpredictably. The spec is a design document; the prompt is its implementation.

Three rules every agent shares

1 · Asymmetry. Prefer a false negative over a false positive. Hiding a working bike costs a rider forty seconds; showing a broken one costs the relationship. The two are not equivalent errors and the system is not allowed to treat them as such.

2 · Uncertainty never resolves in the company's favour. Ambiguous billing defaults to not charging. If the system can't prove the rider owes it, the rider doesn't owe it.

3 · Every automated decision states its reason in plain language at the moment it becomes visible. Not in a settings screen, not in a help centre — in the sentence where the rider first encounters the outcome.

You are a billing assistant for a bike-sharing service. A rider has

What it produced

Fluent, warm, and unusable. It invented remedies that do not exist in the product — a "20% off your next ride" code in two scenarios, a "priority support callback" in another. In four of twelve it asserted facts the telemetry didn't contain: "it looks like the bike had a mechanical fault" when the trace showed only a GPS gap. And when the evidence was genuinely thin it produced a paragraph of apology and no decision at all.

The diagnosis: unbounded action space and no grounding requirement. "Resolve fairly" describes a value, not a behaviour. The model filled the gap with the most statistically plausible customer-service response, which is exactly what a customer-service corpus would predict and exactly not what this product does.

V2Structured output + grounding requirement8 / 12 passed
What I added

A strict JSON output contract, an enumerated action list, and a rule that every claim must cite an event ID present in the telemetry array. The hallucinated discount codes disappeared immediately — an enumerated action space is the single highest-leverage constraint in this whole prompt.

What it still got wrong — and this one is a design failure, not a model failure

Four scenarios failed on ambiguity. Given incomplete telemetry, V2 defaulted to no_action — because "I cannot prove a failure occurred" reads, to a model with no other instruction, as "therefore no adjustment." That is silently resolving uncertainty in the company's favour, the exact behaviour my own spec forbids. The rule existed in my document and nowhere in the prompt.

It also wrote for the wrong reader: "Adjustment applied: lock_state_transition anomaly detected at t=1342 with confidence 0.71." Grounded, accurate, and meaningless to a person standing next to a bike.

The shipped prompt
billing_resolution.system.txt · v3 — running in the prototype
# ROLE
You adjudicate fare disputes for a shared e-bike service. You are not
a support agent. You do not converse. You return one decision per ride.

# WHAT YOU RECEIVE
A single ride's telemetry: an ordered event array (GPS fixes, lock
open/close pings, battery readings, fare ticks), the fare ledger, and
any flags raised by the readiness or battery agents during that ride.
Nothing else. If a fact is not in that array, you do not know it.

# ACTIONS — you may return exactly one
  adjust     reduce the fare by a stated amount, in INR
  no_action  the fare stands as charged
  escalate   a human reviews this; the rider is not charged meanwhile

# RULES
1. Ground everything. Every claim in your output must cite the id of
   an event present in the array. No event, no claim. You may not
   infer a mechanical cause the telemetry does not record.

2. Asymmetry. Uncertainty never resolves in the company's favour.
   If the evidence is incomplete, ambiguous, or contradictory, you
   return escalate — never no_action. no_action is only for rides
   where the telemetry positively shows a normal ride.

3. Never increase a fare. There is no upward adjustment. If the data
   suggests the rider was undercharged, return no_action.

4. Bounded remedies. You may adjust the fare. You may not offer
   credits, discounts, callbacks, priority anything, or future rides.
   These do not exist. Referring to them is a failure, not a kindness.

5. Stop-the-meter events are already handled. The fare stopped at the
   failure automatically. You are deciding what happens to the time
   already charged, not whether to stop charging.

# RIDER MESSAGE — style contract
- Two sentences maximum. Plain language. No apology.
- State what happened, then state the correction. In that order.
- Use the rider's own frame of reference: clock time, not timestamps;
  minutes, not event ids; rupees, not decimals of a fare unit.
- Never name an agent, a confidence value, or a system component.
- Good:  "The lock didn't register when you finished at 2:14 pm, so
          we've removed the extra 9 minutes from your fare."
- Bad:   "We're so sorry! Our system detected a lock_state anomaly."

# OUTPUT — JSON only, no prose outside it
{
  "action":       "adjust" | "no_action" | "escalate",
  "amount_inr":   number,            // 0 unless action is adjust
  "evidence":     [event_id, ...],   // must be non-empty
  "reason_internal": string,         // for the audit log, not the rider
  "rider_message":   string          // obeys the style contract above
}
The one that still fails, and why I shipped it anyway

Scenario 9: telemetry clearly showed a normal ride, and V3 escalated rather than returning no_action. Over-caution — the model reading Rule 2 more strictly than I intended.

I accepted it. An over-escalating billing agent sends a small number of clean rides to a human queue. An under-escalating one silently charges riders for failures it couldn't prove. Those are not symmetrical costs, and the entire product is built on the claim that they aren't. Tightening Rule 2 to eliminate scenario 9 would have moved the failure to the side that costs the rider — which would be the wrong fix, correctly executed.

Artefact 13 · Context engineering
What the billing agent is told — and what it is deliberately denied
The denials are the design

In context

Everything needed to adjudicate this ride, and nothing that identifies whose ride it is.

  • The ordered event array — GPS fixes, lock pings, battery readings, fare ticks, each with an id the output must cite.
  • The fare ledger for this ride only.
  • Flags raised by the other agents during this ride — so a dispute is never re-investigated from scratch.
  • The rider's reported issue category, if she used "Report an issue" — one of four fixed options, attached with telemetry automatically.

Withheld on purpose

Each of these would make the model's output better calibrated and the product worse.

  • The rider's name. Nothing about adjudication changes if she's called Ananya. Its only effect would be on the tone of the message.
  • Ride history and lifetime value. A model that knows this rider has taken 400 rides will resolve differently from one told she has taken two. That is fairness priced by loyalty, and it is indefensible.
  • Prior dispute count. The fastest way to build a system that punishes people for having had things go wrong before.
  • Wallet balance. No adjudication should ever be influenced by whether the rider can afford the outcome.
  • Anything about the fleet's margins. The model has no business knowing what a refund costs.
Why this is the section I'd defend first in a design review

Every item on the right would improve a metric. Withholding them is a product decision about what kind of system this is, enforced at the context layer where it can't be quietly undone by a later prompt tweak. An agent that cannot see who you are cannot treat you differently for being you.

A prototype you can break on purpose.

Working code, not a clickable flat. Enter a destination and the readiness rule actually runs; toggle a fault and the failure states fire for real. The panel on the right exposes what the agents decided and why — which is the only way to review an invisible system.

Then toggle "Lock won't confirm at end" and finish a ride: that is Ananya's original bad experience, redesigned so the cost lands on the system instead of on her.

The agent inspector is a design decision, not a debug tool

An invisible AI layer has a reviewability problem: if the whole thesis is that the rider never sees the intelligence, how does anyone — a stakeholder, an engineer, a hiring manager — verify it's doing anything at all? The inspector panel is the answer. It sits outside the phone frame, shows each agent's state as silent, surfaced or idle, prints the live readiness arithmetic, and lists every hidden bike with the reason it failed. The rider's experience stays silent; the system's reasoning stays inspectable. Those are different audiences and they get different surfaces.

The fault-injection panel exists for the same reason. A prototype that only demonstrates the happy path is arguing the easy case. Five toggles, five real failure branches, no scripted demo path.

Artefact 17 · AI-generated code, and what I changed
Four corrections worth showing
The model wrote most of this prototype.
None of it shipped unreviewed.

Working this way is fast and it is not passive. The pattern that held throughout: the model writes code that satisfies the description; I catch the places where the description wasn't the requirement. These four are the ones that changed the product rather than the syntax.

01 · readiness — a boolean is not enoughMy change
- function ready(bike, trip) {
-   return bike.range >= required(trip) && bike.lock === 'ok'
-       && bike.gps === 'ok' && bike.svc <= 14;
- }
+ function ready(b) {
+   const need = req(S.dest ? S.dest.km : 0);
+   const reasons = [];
+   if (b.range < need)   reasons.push('range ' + b.range.toFixed(1) + ' km < required ' + need.toFixed(1) + ' km');
+   if (b.lock === 'flaky') reasons.push('lock state unconfirmed on last close');
+   if (b.gps === 'drift')  reasons.push('GPS drift beyond tolerance');
+   if (b.svc > 14)         reasons.push('days since service = ' + b.svc);
+   return { ok: reasons.length === 0, reasons, need };
+ }
Why

The boolean is correct code and the wrong shape. Three things downstream need the reason, not the verdict: the inspector panel, the ops dashboard where staff see why a bike was flagged, and the F1 empty state that has to name the check out loud. A model can't know that a design requirement three screens away dictates a function's return type. That's the judgement half of the job.

02 · map render — a correctness bug that broke the core promiseMy change
// AI's version: render the fleet, then remove what fails.
- FLEET.forEach(b => drawPin(b));
- FLEET.filter(b => !ready(b).ok).forEach(b => removePin(b));

// Mine: clear first, then render only what survives.
+ const list = cleared();          // filter, then draw
+ list.forEach(b => drawPin(b));
Why this one mattered more than it looks

Functionally identical after one frame. But for that frame the rider sees a bike appear and then vanish — and the entire product promise is "if it's on the map, it can make this trip." A sixteen-millisecond flash of a bike that isn't real is the product contradicting itself. I only caught it because I was watching for that specific promise; a code review looking for bugs would have passed it.

03 · marginal threshold — a designed state that could never fireMy change
- const marginal = b => ready(b).ok && b.range < ready(b).need * 1.2;
+ const marginal = b => { const r = ready(b); return r.ok && b.range < r.need * 1.6; };
Why

1.2× was a plausible-looking number and it made the marginal-trip notice unreachable: the readiness rule already requires 1.5× the trip distance, so a bike that clears the gate at 1.5× and fails "marginal" at 1.2× describes an empty set. A carefully designed screen, specified in the wireframes, that no rider could ever see. Running the scripted scenarios is what surfaced it — and fixing it meant putting the number into the behaviour spec, not just into the code, so the three layers agree.

04 · fault injection — a scaffolding decision that became a design oneMy change
// AI's version: read fault flags from the URL at load.
- const faults = new URLSearchParams(location.search);
- if (faults.has('unlockFail')) { ... }

// Mine: live togglable state, flipped mid-flow.
+ S.faults = { noBikes:false, unlockFail:false, dieMidRide:false,
+              lockStuck:false, gpsGap:false };
+ document.querySelectorAll('[data-f]').forEach(b => b.onclick = () => {
+   S.faults[b.dataset.f] = !S.faults[b.dataset.f];   // break it mid-ride

Why

URL flags mean choosing the failure before the ride starts, which is exactly the wrong ergonomics — real failures arrive in the middle of things. Making faults live-togglable means a reviewer can kill the battery while the ride is running and watch the fare stop, the adjustment pre-apply and the billing agent's state flip in the inspector. The demo of the failure states became the argument for them.

13 — Where it broke

The interesting failures weren't in one layer. They were between them.

Four things broke under testing. Three were contained to a single layer. The fourth is the one worth the section.

01
Broke in: design · Fixed in: design

Destination-first stranded anyone without a destination

Making the destination the entry point is what gives every downstream screen a real promise instead of a generic one. It also meant that a rider who just wanted to look at what was nearby had nowhere to go — my first flow had no path past that screen.

Fixed by adding a "just browsing" mode that applies the 2 km floor only, with a banner that says exactly that: "Showing bikes with at least 2 km of range. Add a destination for a better match." The rule I held to is that the product never pretends a floor-based check is a trip-based one.

02
Broke in: the prompt · Fixed in: the prompt

V2 resolved ambiguity in the company's favour

My behaviour spec had said, since before any prompt existed, that uncertainty must never favour the company. V2 defaulted to no_action on ambiguous telemetry in four of twelve scenarios — because a rule written in a design document has no effect on a model that was never given it.

Fixed by writing the asymmetry into the prompt as an explicit branch with its own action: ambiguity returns escalate, and no_action is reserved for rides the telemetry positively shows were normal. The lesson generalises past this project: a value that isn't in the prompt isn't in the product.

03
Broke in: code · Fixed in: code

The map contradicted the product's one promise for a frame

Render-then-remove showed a bike and took it away. Sixteen milliseconds of the system saying something it had spent the entire architecture trying not to say.

Fixed by computing the cleared set before drawing anything. Trivial as a patch, and the only reason it was caught is that I was reviewing against a promise rather than against the spec.

04
Broke in: all three at once · Fixed in: design + spec + code

A designed screen that no rider could ever reach

The marginal-trip notice — the one place the whole product lets an agent speak up about a judgement call — never fired. Not once, in any scenario.

The cause was distributed across three layers, and no single layer was wrong. The wireframes specified the state and described when it should appear in prose. The behaviour specification said "surfaced only when marginal" and gave no number. The code picked 1.2× as a plausible-looking threshold. But readiness already gates at 1.5×, so "clears at 1.5× and is marginal below 1.2×" describes an empty set. Every layer was internally consistent and the product still had a hole in it.

The fix had to touch all three: the threshold moved to 1.6× in the code, the number went into the behaviour specification so the code is no longer its own source of truth, and the inspector panel now prints the marginal calculation so the state is visible during a run instead of being inferred from its absence.

This is the failure mode specific to designing AI products, and it is why the three artefacts have to be maintained as one thing. A behaviour spec that describes intent without numbers, and code that supplies numbers without intent, will agree with each other right up until you check.

15 — What this project taught me

Designing with AI is mostly deciding what not to hand over.

01

The restraint is the skill.

Four agents, one language model, and a written list of the things that stay deterministic — pricing, geofencing, payment state. Anyone can add a model to a feature. Being able to say why a capability isn't AI is what separates a product from a demo, and it's the first question I'd expect from anyone reviewing this seriously.

02

A value that isn't in the prompt isn't in the product.

"Never resolve ambiguity in the company's favour" sat in my behaviour specification for weeks and had precisely no effect on the system's behaviour until it existed as an explicit branch with its own action. Design documents don't constrain models. Prompts do — which makes prompt-writing a design responsibility, not an implementation detail to hand off.

03

The dangerous bugs live between the layers.

The marginal-trip notice failed while the wireframe, the spec and the code were each internally correct. Nothing was broken; they simply weren't the same artefact. Working across design, prompts and code means the integration is the deliverable — and the only thing that catches these is running scenarios against a written standard.

04

Use AI hardest where it can argue with you.

The most valuable thing the model produced wasn't a feature or a screen — it was "you would not draw retention conclusions from this data." It cost me the claim I most wanted to make. I use models for volume, variations and first drafts, but the highest-leverage prompt in this project was the one that made agreement expensive.

05

Failure states are the product.

Five of seventeen screens, designed at the same fidelity as the happy path, because trust is built or destroyed in exactly those five. Most apps hand failure a generic toast and a support number. A product whose entire thesis is reliability cannot treat its own failures as an afterthought.

06

Invisible systems still have to be inspectable.

The inspector panel started as a debugging convenience and became the thing that makes the work reviewable at all. If the rider never sees the intelligence, someone has to — a stakeholder, an engineer, an auditor. Designing that second surface is part of designing an AI product, not an extra.
